# Chapter 1: Rain Over the High City

The autumn rain was coming down hard and steady over Aethelgard's dark slate roofs, drumming incessantly against the High City's copper gutters. High up in the metropolis, the downpour washed clean the brass balustrades and the faces of archbishops carved into stone, but deep within the Grand Archives, the air remained unchanged—heavy, still, and steeped in centuries of history.

Beneath basalt vaults rising over sixty feet above the stone floor, a near-monastic silence reigned alongside a dizzying labyrinth of dark oak shelves. Thousands of ledgers, sea charts, and theological compilations stacked their gilded or dust-worn spines across the aisles. It smelled of linseed oil, old rag paper dust, dried parchment, and cold bergamot tea left abandoned on the edge of blotters.

Tucked behind her heavy study desk of solid mahogany, Lira Vance carefully adjusted her double-lens brass monocle. At twenty-four, trapped in a suffocating bureaucratic routine, she felt her mind withering day by day under the iron thumb of the Council of Silence. For the Academy's top scholars, Aethelgard's history was set in stone once and for all: a divine creation born out of chaos, destined for order, discipline, and the controlled extraction of coal.

But Lira knew this official narrative was nothing more than a golden lie engineered to keep the populace obedient.

Down below, beyond the basalt precipice upon which the High City clung, in the dark, damp alleys of the Lower Town, the Amber Cough was taking a harrowing toll. Every morning, parish bells in the working-class quarters tolled for children poisoned by the toxic coal fumes spewed endlessly by the Council's factories. Lira could no longer turn a blind eye to this misery. She could no longer pretend to tally accounting figures while the city choked.

Under the weak, flickering glow of her rapeseed oil lamp, Lira turned the pages of the *Aethelgard Codex*. It was a thick, rectangular volume bound in rough leather, weathered by centuries, its cover debossed with a geometric symbol faded by time. For nearly three hundred years, successive directors of the Archives had relegated this volume to the back of Aisle 42, dismissively cataloging it among old, obsolete star catalogs of no commercial value.

Yet the name of the original depositor appeared in faint watermark script on the flyleaf: Aldous Vance. Her own great-grandfather.

Lira ran her fingertips over the edge of the binding. Beneath the dry surface of the antique leather, she had always sensed an unusual rigidity, as if the cover wrapped around an underlying metal framework. That night, as the October moon cast long bluish shadows across the floor tiles through the pointed stained-glass windows, an intricate detail caught her eye.

She brought her magnifying lens within three millimeters of the bottom margin on page three hundred and forty-two.

« This can't be... », she whispered under her breath, making the small yellow lamp flame flicker.

The grain of the page was entirely different from the vellum or rag paper produced by the Lower Town mills. Interwoven between the yellowed plant fibers were microscopic golden marblings forming a geometric lattice of supernatural precision. Lira cautiously brought the lamp closer. The warmth of the flame and the refraction angle of the light revealed the unthinkable.

The black ink of the celestial script was not made of ordinary soot or gall-tannate. It was a complex emulsion of liquid synthetic amber and pulverized copper dust. And under the combined action of the night chill seeping through the windows and the warmth of her breath, the dark lines began to quiver.

The characters no longer described star positions; they were sliding. The ink strokes stretched, branched out, and adjusted like miniature clockwork gears. The ink was breathing.

An electric, ice-cold shiver ran down Lira's spine. She pulled her dark wool coat tighter around her shoulders, her heart hammering wildly in her chest. This codex was no mere collection of antique prayers or obsolete astronomy: it was a live receiving matrix, a piece of living mechanics crafted a thousand years ago by Precursor engineers.

Every symbol masked a micro-resonant metallic circuit capable of capturing subterranean ground vibrations and decoding their frequencies. And her great-grandfather had not hidden this volume by accident: he had left her a legacy of light in the heart of Aethelgard's night. Lira stared at the moving page, resolved to see this through to the very end, whatever the cost to her own freedom.
